I have owned eleven keyboards. I have been told, by people who love me, that this is too many keyboards. They are right, and this is the one that stopped the run — not because it's perfect, but because every remaining complaint I have is small enough that fixing it would mean buying something worse in a different way.

Four months of daily use, most of it writing rather than gaming. Here's what survived contact with reality.

## The gasket mount is not marketing

I assumed it was. "Gasket mount" appears on the box of almost every enthusiast board now, and the gap between the marketing claim and the felt difference is usually enormous.

It isn't here. The plate sits on rubber strips rather than being screwed directly to the case, and the practical result is that a bottom-out feels like it stops in soft material instead of on aluminium. After a long writing session my fingertips ache noticeably less than they did on my previous tray-mount board. That's not a subtle preference thing — it's the difference between finishing a 3,000-word post comfortably and wanting to stop at 2,000.

Where it matters less: gaming. During play I'm not bottoming out ninety per cent of keystrokes on the same four keys, so the cushioning barely registers.

## Hot-swap changes the economics of the hobby

This is the feature that actually matters, and it's the reason I'd tell someone to spend up to reach it.

Trying a new switch used to mean buying a whole keyboard, or spending an evening with a soldering iron and accepting that a mistake was permanent. With hot-swap sockets it means buying ten switches for the price of a coffee, pulling the old ones out with the included tool, and pushing new ones in. The whole operation takes about four minutes.

I went through three switch types before settling. On a soldered board I'd have settled for the first one and quietly resented it.

One caveat: the sockets are rated for a finite number of insertions — a few hundred, depending on who you ask. That's far beyond what any normal person will do, but if you plan to swap switches weekly, be aware there's a ceiling.

## The stabilisers are the real surprise

Stabilisers are the parts under your long keys — space, enter, backspace, shift. They are also, on almost every keyboard under a certain price, the single worst component. Rattly, hollow, and the reason a board that felt great in a review sounds cheap on your desk.

These come lubed and tuned from the factory. The space bar produces a low thock rather than a metallic rattle, straight out of the box, with no work from me. I checked whether I could improve them with a proper tune and concluded I could not meaningfully.

For context: on my previous board, fixing the stabilisers took two hours, a syringe of dielectric grease, and a YouTube tutorial I had to pause eleven times. Not having to do that is worth real money.

## The knob

I mocked rotary knobs. They looked like a gimmick — a thing added to a spec sheet because it photographs well.

I now use it constantly. Volume by default, but it's remappable, and in my editor it's mapped to undo/redo granularity, which is a strange thing to discover you want. During video editing it scrubs the timeline. It's the control I reach for without looking, which is the actual test of a good physical input.

I was wrong. It's the best part of the board.

## What doesn't hold up

**Weight.** At 1.6 kg it does not move. That's excellent on a desk and disqualifying for anything else. If you were hoping for one keyboard that goes to the office and back, this is not it.

**Software.** Windows-only, and mediocre. The saving grace is that the board stores its own key mapping in onboard memory, so you configure it once on a Windows machine and it works everywhere after that — including the Linux box it now lives on. But if you're Mac- or Linux-only with no Windows access at all, you're stuck with the default layout or a community tool.

**RGB zones.** Marketing says four zones. Two are individually addressable; the other two are a single group. Nobody will notice this except the people it will annoy enormously.

## Should you buy it

If you type for a living and you're on a membrane keyboard or a basic mechanical, this is a genuine upgrade and the hot-swap sockets mean you won't need another board to figure out what you like.

If you already own a tuned enthusiast board, this is sideways, not up. Save the money.

If you need portability, look at a 60% or 65% in the same family — same internals, considerably less mass.

**8.5/10.** The half point off is the software and the RGB zone thing. The point off is the weight, which is a genuine limitation rather than a nitpick. Everything that affects typing is close to as good as this price gets.
